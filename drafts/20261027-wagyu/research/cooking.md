# Cooking wagyu: research notes

Collected on 2026-10-27 by a research agent. Serious Eats quotes are agent-verified only: when I tried to re-check them on 2026-10-27, both Serious Eats and the Wayback Machine blocked my fetches. Irie, MAFF and the J-LEC recipe pages were confirmed by fetch. Serious Eats and USDA FSIS block direct fetches, so the agent read those pages as Wayback snapshots, marked "archived" below. Translations from Japanese are marked "tr.".

## 1. Why wagyu cooks differently

- **Fat composition.** Irie (2021), Nihon Chikusan Gakkaiho (National Livestock Breeding Center): "Wagyu fat has high monounsaturated fatty acids composition including oleic acid mainly, and has low melting point… Wagyu fat with low melting point is related to the high texture and rich juiciness." https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/chikusan/92/1/92_1/_article/-char/en (confirmed)
- **Lower than butter.** Same paper, PDF: 「牛脂でも和牛脂肪は例外的に融点が低く，部位や個体によってはバター以下の融点を示す」 (tr.: among beef fats, wagyu fat is an exception with a low melting point, below butter's for some cuts and animals). https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/chikusan/92/1/92_1/_pdf (confirmed)
- **Melting points, approximate.** Read off a chart (Fig. 4, from Irie 2015), so treat them as ranges:
  - beef tallow: ~40–56 °C
  - wagyu subcutaneous fat: ~10–29 °C
  - wagyu intermuscular fat: ~18–37 °C
  - wagyu kidney fat: ~28–45 °C
  - butter: ~25–36 °C

  Marbling (intramuscular) fat is not shown separately.
- **Fatty acids.** Melting points: oleic 13.4 °C, stearic 69.6 °C, palmitic 63.1 °C (Table 1, after Wood et al. 2003). Same PDF. (confirmed)
- **Melts in the mouth.** Olmsted, Serious Eats 2019: "The unsaturated fats in wagyu have a low melting point, below 98.6°F, so they literally melt on your tongue." https://www.seriouseats.com/fake-kobe-wagyu-beef-japanese-steak (archived)
- **Aroma.** J-LEC: "this Wagyu beef aroma comes out strongest when meat is heated to 80 degrees Celsius (176 degrees Fahrenheit), which matches the temperature best suited for sukiyaki." https://jlec-pr.jp/en/beef/beef-tokutyou/ (archived)
- **Doneness.** Gritzer, Serious Eats 2019: "Japanese wagyu doesn't easily dry out, and, if anything, needs a little more heat to begin melting that internal fat." https://www.seriouseats.com/we-ate-super-expensive-steak-so-you-dont-have-to (archived)

## 2. Japanese preparations

- **Sukiyaki, Kansai vs. Kanto.**
  - MAFF: "In Kansai, the meat is first grilled, and then broth and vegetables are added, while in Kanto, especially in homes, boiling the broth before adding both meat and vegetable[s]." https://www.maff.go.jp/e/policies/market/k_ryouri/search_menu/4477/index.html (confirmed)
  - JNTO: "in the Kansai region beef is browned with sugar before adding liquid ingredients and vegetables, while in Kanto the liquid ingredients are mixed together to make a warishita sauce." https://www.japan.travel/en/sg/story/kanto-vs-kansai-battle-cuisines/ (confirmed)
  - J-LEC: "Sirloin, Ribloin", "thinly sliced"; melt beef tallow in the pot first. https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/themes/jlec/beef-recipe/en/download/sukiyaki.pdf (archived)
- **Shabu-shabu.**
  - J-LEC: "Chuck roll, Top-round"; "When the water temperature reaches about 80 degrees °C, blanch the meat in the pot, piece by piece." https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/themes/jlec/beef-recipe/en/download/syabusyabu.pdf (archived)
  - Another J-LEC recipe: "Wagyu sirloin (thinly sliced about 1.5 mm thick)". https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/beef-Art_of_Japanese_Wagyu_Cooking_Part9.pdf (archived)
- **Yakiniku.** J-LEC: "Shoulder clod, Chuck roll, Top-round, Gooseneck round"; "Chop meat into bite-size pieces… Roast both sides of the meat on a grill." https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/themes/jlec/beef-recipe/en/download/yakiniku.pdf (archived)
- **Teppanyaki.** Kikkoman: "Teppanyaki literally means 'grilled on an iron plate.'" Overseas it means "specialty restaurants where steaks and vegetables are prepared on a griddle right in front of diners." https://www.kikkoman.com/en/culture/foodforum/trends-in-taste/38-3_teppan-style_tabletop_cooking.html (confirmed). No source found on wagyu cut or thickness.
- **Aburi nigiri.** J-LEC: "Flame-seared (aburi) Wagyu sirloin rivals fatty tuna (toro)… Slice Wagyu sirloin thinly into about 8 slices… Lightly sear the scored top surface… using a propane burner." https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/beef-Art_of_Japanese_Wagyu_Cooking_Part10.pdf (archived)
  - Irie: 「和牛肉は例外とされ，寿司などの冷食にも向く」 (tr.: wagyu is the exception among beef in suiting cold dishes such as sushi). (confirmed)
- **Gyukatsu.** J-LEC: "Cut meat into two 1.5cm (0.6 in) thick slices… deep fry the cutlets [at 170 °C] for 2 minutes." Recommended cuts: "Sirloin, Fillet". It also suggests a "Beef Cutlet Sandwich". https://jlec-pr.jp/wp-content/themes/jlec/beef-recipe/en/download/katsu.pdf (archived)
  - The restaurant style of gyukatsu (rare inside, finished on a hot stone) is unconfirmed.

## 3. Western methods

All quotes are from Gritzer (archived) unless another source is named.

- **Thickness.** "The Japanese wagyu was cut much thinner, at about half an inch thick, while the American beef was cut into steaks that were between one and one-and-a-half inches thick."
- **Cast iron.** "The steaks seared in a cast iron pan all turned out well… no reverse sear, no sous vide. The Japanese steaks are too thin to warrant those treatments—once you have your sear, the steaks are done internally as well."
- **Reverse sear.** López-Alt: "the method doesn't work very well for steaks thinner than an inch and a half or so." https://www.seriouseats.com/reverse-seared-steak-recipe (archived)
- **Sous vide.** López-Alt: "I recommend cooking very fatty or coarse pieces of beef to the cooler side of medium"; medium is "135°F/57°C". https://www.seriouseats.com/food-lab-complete-guide-to-sous-vide-steak (archived)
- **Grilling.**
  - The plancha "caused severe flareups, especially with the fattier Japanese beef"; "Holy Grail Steak Company doesn't recommend grilling wagyu… due to flareups"; small pieces over binchotan "came out great".
  - Irie: high MUFA is a drawback in high-heat grilling: 「高温の焼成調理では欠点」. (confirmed)
- **Doneness.** "the Japanese wagyu was at its best cooked to about medium, while all the American steaks hit their peak around medium-rare."
- **Resting.**
  - López-Alt: "Traditionally cooked steaks need to rest… for five to 10 minutes."
  - His reverse-sear recipe: "There's no need for a resting period."

## 4. Portions

- **A5.** "For the Japanese A5 wagyu, plan to serve about one to two ounces of meat per person. That means a 13-ounce steak can feed anywhere from six to 12 people!" Vendors suggest about "four bites per person." (Gritzer)
- **Always small.** Olmsted: "Ultra-rich, wagyu is always served in small quantities."
- **Thin-sliced dishes.** J-LEC: 500 g of sukiyaki serves four; 200 g of yakiniku serves two.

## 5. American wagyu vs. Japanese A5

All quotes are from Gritzer unless another source is named.

- **Eats like ordinary steak.** American wagyu steaks "eat much more like standard steaks… we were just as happy cutting into the aged prime ribeye."
- **Portions.** "The American wagyu and Black Angus steaks… can each feed one to two people per 16-ounce portion."
- **Cooking.** Thicker American steaks did well "simply flipping them back and forth until… a nice medium-rare." Cut into small pieces and grilled, American wagyu "tended to overcook and grow chewy."
- **Marbling score.** Olmsted: "most American-raised wagyu or hybrids would score between six and nine [on Japan's 12-point marbling scale], while the USDA's highest grade, Prime, rings in at around four."

## 6. Food safety (USDA FSIS)

- **Steaks.** "Beef, Pork, Veal and Lamb Steaks, Chops and Roasts: 145 degrees F (62.8 degrees C) and allow to rest for at least 3 minutes." https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart (archived 2026-10-04)
- **Ground meat.** "Ground Meats: 160 degrees F (71.1 degrees C)."
- **Conflict with chef advice.** Medium-rare and medium (130–135 °F) are below USDA's 145 °F steak minimum.

## Gaps

- No peer-reviewed melting point specifically for marbling fat.
- Serious Eats pieces are from 2019–2022; there is no 2026 primary source on technique.
- The Kobe Beef association FAQ has no cooking content.
